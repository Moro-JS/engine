# @morojs/engine 1.2.5

Memory under flood, on a host with memory to spare. 1.2.4 bounded what the
engine holds for its peers and was measured on a machine whose kernel
happened to bound the rest. On a larger host the same floods had a 4 GiB
container killed, with the engine and the runtime at an eighth of it. Four
things were found there and are corrected:

- What the kernel queues for a server's connections to read had no bound at
  all. On Linux it now has a budget, as the engine's own buffers have.
- A response waiting in the write queue held up to twice the memory the
  budget counted for it.
- Responses that peers ask for and never read were bounded only by a sweep
  that could take many seconds, and not at all while the peer kept sending.
  A thread far over its budget now closes those connections within a
  second.
- On a thread out of buffer memory, a connection went on being answered
  until it had 256 KiB of responses it had not taken. It now waits at 16 KiB,
  or less on a crowded thread.

One capability is new (`socketBudget`) and four fields of
`probe().bufferBudget` (`connections`, `sockets`, `receiveBuffer`,
`resized`). No option is new: `memoryBudget` governs all of it. No export or
option is removed or renamed.

Who should update:

- Anyone on Linux who serves clients they do not control, more than two
  connections per MiB of the process's memory at a time. See "The socket
  budget".
- Anyone who serves large responses to clients they do not control. See
  "Responses nobody reads".
- A server whose connections fit - up to 8,192 of them in a 4 GiB container,
  131,072 with 64 GiB - and whose threads stay within their buffer budget
  runs as on 1.2.4: no socket option is set, and one comparison per accepted
  connection is all that was added to its path.

## Changes you may notice

Each item says who notices and what to do.

- **On a crowded server, a large body from a peer on the same host arrives
  slowly.** Crowded means more connections than fit the socket budget with
  the kernel's own receive buffer each: more than two per MiB of the memory
  the process may use, and buffers go below 64 KiB past four per MiB. From
  there, a body larger than the connection's receive buffer, sent over the
  loopback interface, arrives one buffer at a time, about five a second:
  1 MiB took 6.7 s through 32 KiB buffers and 13 s through 16 KiB. A request
  or body that fits the buffer is served as before, and so is everything
  from a peer on a network. Who notices: a server behind a proxy on the same
  host, holding that many connections at once, that takes uploads. What to
  do: give the container the memory its connection count needs, raise
  `memoryBudget` (the socket budget is twice it), or have the proxy keep a
  pool of upstream connections instead of one per request.
- **A connection that was open while its thread was crowded has a receive
  buffer of 128 KiB for the rest of its life**, where one never touched may
  be grown by the kernel under a fast upload. Who notices: long uploads over
  fast, long-distance links on connections that outlived a crowd. Nothing to
  do.
- **`memoryBudget: Infinity` turns the socket budget off as well.** Nothing
  to do unless you set it and want the new bound: give `memoryBudget` a
  number.
- **A connection that is owed more than 16 KiB of response and takes none of
  it for half a second is closed, while its thread holds twice its buffer
  budget.** Until now such a connection went at the sweep, two sweep
  intervals after it last moved a byte in either direction - up to eight
  seconds at the default timeouts, and never while it kept sending. Taking
  means acknowledging any of it, or opening an HTTP/2 window for it: a
  client that is reading, however slowly, is not affected, and neither is
  any thread below twice its share. Who notices: clients that ask for large
  responses and stop reading, on a server that is out of buffer memory.
  Nothing to do.
- **On a thread over its buffer budget, a connection that has 16 KiB of
  responses waiting is not answered further until it takes them** - the
  write high-water mark (256 KiB by default) applies only while the thread
  has room. On a thread with more connections than half its share has
  16 KiB for, the figure is each connection's part of that half, 4 KiB at
  the least (8 KiB at 16,384 connections a thread, with the default budget
  in a 4 GiB container). Who notices: a client that pipelines requests
  faster than it reads responses, on a server that is out of buffer memory;
  its requests wait, as they did at the higher mark, and none is refused.
  A request of up to 16 KiB is still never refused for the budget. Nothing
  to do.

## The socket budget

The buffer budget of 1.2.4 counts what the engine holds: requests it has
read and not yet handed on, responses its peers have not taken. What it has
not read yet is somewhere else. Every connection has a receive buffer in the
kernel - 128 KiB to begin with at Linux defaults - and three things are true
of it:

- The peer decides how full it is. No limit of the server's is consulted
  when a packet arrives; the limits are applied when the server reads.
- A server reads a socket when its turn comes. Under a flood - and since
  1.2.4 a loop turn ends after 50 ms - most sockets are waiting, and peers
  that send faster than the server reads keep their buffers full.
- The memory is charged to the server's cgroup like its own. When the cgroup
  is full the kernel does not refuse the next packet; the process is killed
  at its next page fault.

So the number of connections, and nothing they are allowed to send, decides
how much memory that is: 32,768 connections can hold 4 GiB of it. In a 4 GiB
container with 32,768 connections each sending one request with a single
enormous header - which the engine refuses with 431 as soon as it reads it -
socket buffers were 2.9 GiB of a 3.5 GiB peak, and the engine and the runtime
together 0.5 GiB.

The receive buffers of a thread's connections therefore have a budget of
their own: twice the thread's buffer budget, which is a quarter of the
memory the process may use unless `memoryBudget` is set, divided among the
threads that serve.

- **While the connections fit, nothing is done.** `tcp_rmem`'s default is
  read once; a thread whose connections fit its share with that much each
  pays one comparison per accept and has no socket option set. The kernel
  goes on sizing those buffers itself.
- **Past that, each connection's buffer is its part of the budget**: the
  largest power of two that fits, 64 KiB at most and 16 KiB at least, set
  with `SO_RCVBUF`. A connection gets it when it is accepted. The ones that
  were already open get it at the accept that halves the part - one pass
  over n sockets each time n doubles - and the sweep sets any socket that is
  not where it should be. The kernel then advertises a smaller window, and
  a peer can keep that much queued and no more.
- **When there is room again, buffers are raised** at the sweep, one step
  short of what fits so that a connection count moving about a boundary does
  not set every socket at every sweep, and with no crowd left to 128 KiB.

The cost is in "Changes you may notice", and it is narrow for a reason that
is the kernel's: a sender does not split a segment to fit a window smaller
than the segment, it waits for its probe timer. Segments are 1.5 KiB on a
network and 64 KiB on the loopback interface, so only a loopback peer, and
only for a body larger than the buffer, is paced. Other ways to bound the
same memory were weighed and left: counting each socket's queue and closing
the largest holders takes a system call per socket and acts at the sweep,
long after a flood has filled the cgroup; `TCP_WINDOW_CLAMP` is rewritten by
the kernel's own sizing on several kernel versions; a smaller segment size
is fixed at the handshake and would cost every loopback connection always.

What it does not bound: 16 KiB is the least a buffer is set to, so past one
connection per 16 KiB of the budget it is `maxConnections` that bounds the
kernel's share; the kernel charges a queued segment by its allocation, not
its payload, and a queue may pass its buffer by a segment (1.3 GiB measured
against a 1 GiB budget); send buffers are left alone - what a peer does not
read is in the engine's write queue past the first buffer, where the buffer
budget counts it. macOS and Windows are unchanged.

`probe().bufferBudget` gains `connections` (the thread's open connections),
`sockets` (its share, `Infinity` without one), `receiveBuffer` (what each
connection's buffer is held to now, 0 while the kernel's own fits) and
`resized` (how often a buffer was set).

## Queued writes

A response the socket does not take at once is queued in a buffer of its
own, and the budget counts the bytes in it. The storage was often twice
that, in two ways:

- The buffer was the one the response was built in - a cork buffer grown by
  doubling, or one whose sent part had been erased - with all of its
  capacity. A write that reaches the queue is now cut to what it holds when
  it holds a quarter more than that.
- When a socket took part of a queued write and no more, the part it took
  stayed allocated with the rest. For a peer that has stopped reading that
  is for the life of the connection: the front of its queue went into the
  kernel's send buffer, and the write it belonged to sat at the head with a
  few kilobytes left. Now, when at least half of a write has gone and 64 KiB
  or less is left, what is left is kept and the rest freed. A large
  remainder is left alone, so a body being downloaded is not moved each
  time the socket fills.

Under a flood of pipelined requests whose answers nobody read, queued writes
were 549 MiB allocated for 284 MiB counted on each thread; they are 237 MiB
for 216 MiB now.

Both use a new helper (`src/shrink.h`) and not `std::string::shrink_to_fit`,
for a reason found on the way: libstdc++ implements `shrink_to_fit` inside
`#if __cpp_exceptions`, the engine is built with `-fno-exceptions`, and so
on Linux that call compiles to nothing. The engine's thirteen existing calls
to it - the ones that release a parser's, a response's, an HTTP/2 stream's
or a WebSocket message's buffer once it has grown past 16 or 64 KiB - have
therefore never released anything on Linux, while they do on macOS and
Windows. They are left exactly as they are in this release: what they would
free is bounded by the limits on the request, response or message that grew
it and, for a pooled request state, by the 128 KiB a state may keep; and
making them act puts a free and an allocation on the path of every large
upload and response, which wants measuring on its own.

## Responses nobody reads

The buffer budget closes a connection for the requests it is still sending,
never for the responses it is owed where they are queued: a response to a
peer that reads must arrive whatever its size. What is owed and not taken
was left to the sweep, which closes a connection that has moved nothing for
two sweep intervals while its thread is nearly out of room. Two things were
wrong with that as a bound:

- It is slow against what it bounds. A request of a hundred bytes can ask
  for a megabyte, handlers answer as fast as they run, and a sweep interval
  is up to four seconds. 32,768 HTTP/2 connections that each opened 64
  streams with a window of zero had a 4 GiB container at 3.5 GiB of held
  responses within ten seconds, against a budget of 0.5 GiB.
- A read counted as moving. A peer that kept sending - more pipelined
  requests, more HTTP/2 frames - was never still, and never closed.

So while a thread holds twice its share, a clock of 250 ms looks at every
connection that is owed more than it may always hold, and asks one thing:
has its peer taken anything since the last look. Taken is a write that
completed, an engine queue plus kernel send queue that is smaller than it
was, or held-back HTTP/2 bytes that went out because a window opened. What
the peer sends is not asked, and on a connection with responses behind a
window neither is a completed write - its `HEADERS` and its `PING` answers
complete whether or not it ever opens one. A connection that has taken
nothing at two looks running is closed, the one owed most first, until the
thread is back within its share; then the clock stops. Below twice the
share nothing of this runs and the sweep's rule is the only one, as before.

One smaller thing works with it. On a thread over its share, the mark at
which a connection's further requests wait until it takes its responses is
16 KiB, not the write high-water mark - and each connection's part of half
the thread's share when there are more connections than that half has
16 KiB for, 4 KiB at the least. Nothing is refused by it; what a connection
may send before it can be refused is 16 KiB as in 1.2.4, whatever the crowd.

## Why 1.2.4's measurements did not show it

1.2.4 was measured against HttpArena's resilience suite on a machine whose
kernel-wide TCP memory limit (`tcp_mem`, derived from the machine's memory)
was 2.2 GiB. That limit made the kernel hold every socket's buffer down
before the container's own limit was near, and all 41 attacks were survived
there. A host with enough memory has no such limit in reach, and on one the
same build was killed for memory in 11 of the 41. The limit is lifted for
the measurements of this release.

## Measured against floods

HttpArena's resilience suite: 41 attacks, 32,768 connections each, against a
server in a 4 GiB container with one core and its sibling. On the suite's
own host 1.2.4 was killed for memory in 11 of them. The figures below are
from a second machine with its kernel-wide TCP memory limit raised to 6 GiB,
where 1.2.4 reaches the same state: peak memory in MiB, of 4,096.

| Attack | 1.2.4 | 1.2.5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `header-bomb` | 4,045 | 1,251 |
| `decompression-bomb` | 3,920 | 902 |
| `pipelining-flood` | killed in one run of two, 3,664 in the other | 2,788 |
| `window-stall` | 3,763 to 3,866 | 2,506 |

All 41 are survived on 1.2.5 with no health check missed; the highest peak
of the run is 2,846 MiB (`decomp-codec-bypass`).

## Performance

Engine instructions per request, counted with callgrind on Linux arm64 on
plain (not profile-guided) builds of 1.2.4 and of this release, epoll
transport, one connection:

| Workload | 1.2.4 | 1.2.5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| keep-alive request | 2,490 | 2,493 |
| pipelined request (16 per write) | 2,001 | 2,000 |
| a connection per request | 3,166 | 3,175 |

50,000 keep-alive connections held open in a 2 GiB container with two
threads - twelve times what fits its socket budget, so every socket is at
16 KiB - and one request sent over each: opened in 1.1 s on both releases,
every request answered, p99 3.9 ms against 3.5, memory 288 MiB against 290.
