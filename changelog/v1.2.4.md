# @morojs/engine 1.2.4

Corrections to 1.2.2 and 1.2.3. A pooled request state no longer keeps the
buffers a peer grew. With `http2` on, a reply an engine route had already
given is no longer dropped when the line behind it is not HTTP. The
request-target rules 1.2.2 began are complete, and the same on HTTP/1.1 and
HTTP/2. `requestTimeoutMs` and `idleTimeoutMs` no longer act before their
time has passed. A TLS connection now ends as a plaintext one does - its
`close_notify`, the FIN, and a wait of at most two seconds for the peer's
close - so a response that ends the connection is no longer lost to a reset
when the peer is still sending.

And two defences against load that no single connection's limit sees. What
all connections hold in buffers together is bounded (`memoryBudget`, a
eighth of the memory the process may use unless set), and on Linux a loop
turn has a time slice, so a flood of ordinary requests no longer keeps a
server from answering the client that has just connected.

One serve option is new (`memoryBudget`), one capability (`bufferBudget`)
and three `probe()` fields (`requestStates`, `bufferBudget`, `turns`). No
export or option is removed or renamed.

Who should update:

- Anyone on 1.2.3 who serves clients they do not control. Since 1.2.3 the
  parser, the request snapshot and the staging ring are a state that
  connections take from a pool and give back, and a state kept the capacity
  of all of its buffers. The ones that hold a request's path, query and
  header fields grow to what the peer sends, each up to `maxHeadSize`, and
  there are eighteen sets of them in a state once requests are pipelined. A
  client could grow them, disconnect, and leave them in the pool for as long
  as the server ran: 200 connections that each sent 3.2 MB of requests with
  60,000-byte paths, queries and header values left 25.2 MB behind with
  batched dispatch and 12.8 MB without, where 1.2.2 kept 0.5 MB. What could
  be left was bounded - the pool keeps 32 states after a sweep, about 110 MB
  of such buffers at the default `maxHeadSize` - and it was memory, nothing
  a request could read. See "What a pooled request state holds".
- Anyone on 1.2.2 or 1.2.3 with `http2` on, a cleartext listener, and static
  or parameter routes (`setStaticRoute`, `setParamRoute`): a connection that
  sent a request to such a route and then a line that is not HTTP/1 was
  closed without the response to the request it had sent. See "Replies
  dropped behind an engine route".
- Anyone who sets `requestTimeoutMs` or `idleTimeoutMs` to a few seconds or
  less, on 1.2.3 or earlier: both could act up to one sweep interval early, which
  for a timeout of two seconds or less is up to half of it. A request that
  took 800 ms to arrive under `requestTimeoutMs: 1000` could be answered
  408. See "Receive timeouts".

- Anyone who serves HTTPS or WSS, on any release with TLS: a response that
  ends the connection (`Connection: close`, HTTP/1.0, 400/408/413/431, a
  WebSocket Close, an HTTP/2 `GOAWAY`) could be lost when the client was
  still sending, because the connection was closed right behind its
  `close_notify` and the client's bytes were answered with a reset. An
  upload over the body limit could show up in the client as a connection
  reset instead of a 413. See "The end of a TLS connection".

- Anyone who serves clients they do not control, on any release. Every
  limit the engine had was a limit on one connection, and many connections
  that each stay within theirs could have the process hold more memory than
  it has: in a 4 GiB container, 32,768 connections that each sent a body of
  under a megabyte had 1.2.3 killed for memory within seconds. And a few
  thousand connections that each sent ordinary requests as fast as they
  could made a loop turn take seconds, during which nothing else was
  answered. See "The buffer budget" and "A loop that falls behind".

## Changes you may notice

Each item says who notices and what to do.

- **A request that needs buffer memory the process has none left of is
  answered 503 and its connection closed**, where it used to be buffered.
  The budget is an eighth of the memory the process may use (the cgroup
  limit of a container, the machine's memory otherwise), for what all
  connections hold in engine buffers together; a request of up to 16 KiB is
  never refused for it. A WebSocket whose message is being reassembled is
  closed with 1013 in the same situation, and an HTTP/2 connection with
  `GOAWAY`. A response is never cut for it: what a handler gives a peer
  that reads it is delivered whatever its size, as before. Who: servers
  that at some moment hold more than that eighth in request bodies still
  arriving and responses not yet read - which includes a thread that is
  sending responses larger than its share: until they have gone out, the
  requests of more than 16 KiB that arrive on its other connections are the
  ones refused. What a buffered
  byte costs the process is two or three times what the engine counts (the
  copy a handler is given, the allocator, the kernel's socket buffers), so
  a server past that point is on its way to running out, and on 1.2.3 the
  next thing it met was the kernel's OOM killer. Do: nothing, or
  set `memoryBudget` (bytes for the process; `Infinity` for no budget). A
  thread's share is never below the largest message its servers accept, so
  a single request that was accepted before is accepted now.
- **A connection that holds buffer memory and moves nothing for a second is
  closed while the budget is nearly spent** (less than an eighth of a
  thread's share free), unless a handler holds its request. Who: peers that
  send part of a large body, or ask for a large response and stop reading,
  at a moment when the server has no memory to spare; they used to be held
  until `requestTimeoutMs` or `responseTimeoutMs`. A peer that is reading
  is not one of them, with or without `responseTimeoutMs`, however slowly
  the socket takes from the engine: before a connection with a response
  queued is closed for this, the kernel is asked whether the peer has
  acknowledged anything since the sweep before (Linux and macOS; on Windows
  the engine's own queue is all there is to look at). A peer whose own TCP
  stack acknowledges nothing for a whole second can still be taken for one
  that has stopped, at such a moment (`docs/API.md`, "Memory").
- **On Linux, when the sockets ready in one loop turn ask for more than
  50 ms of work, they are no longer all served in that turn.** The turn
  ends, timers and new connections get their turn, and the sockets not
  reached are served in the turns that follow, connections that have just
  arrived ahead of the ones that were served a moment ago. Nothing is
  dropped. Who: nobody on a server that keeps up (a turn that fits in 50 ms
  is served as before). On a server that does not, responses arrive in a
  different order across connections - never within one - and a timer or a
  new client no longer waits for the whole backlog. `probe().turns` shows
  it happening.

- **A TLS connection stays open for up to two seconds after its last
  response, until the client has closed its side.** The server sends its
  `close_notify` and its FIN with that response, as before, and then waits
  for the client's FIN instead of closing at once; a client that closes on
  seeing the server's close - every browser and HTTP library does - ends the
  wait at once, and one that never closes is cut at two seconds. It is what
  a plaintext connection has done since 1.1.6. Who notices: nobody on the
  wire, except that such responses now arrive; a server under
  `maxConnections` counts a closing TLS connection for those up to two
  seconds, as it counts a closing plaintext one. What the client sends in
  that time is dropped without being decrypted.

- **A request-target that has none of the four forms is 400** (RFC 9112
  §3.2). A target is origin-form, which begins with `/`; absolute-form, a
  scheme followed by `://` (`GET http://host/path`), which is taken and
  handed over as it always was; authority-form, for `CONNECT`, likewise; or
  `*`, for `OPTIONS`. `GET abc`, `GET *x`, `GET ?q` and `GET host:80/path`
  used to be handed to the application with `abc`, `*x`, an empty string and
  `host:80/path` as the path. Who: clients that leave the leading `/` off,
  typically hand-written ones; no browser or HTTP library does. Do: send the
  `/`. For the application this means a path it is given begins with `/`,
  unless the request was `OPTIONS *`, a `CONNECT`, or carried an absolute
  URI; a static or parameter route registered under a text that does not
  begin with `/` (other than `*` for `OPTIONS`) was reachable and no longer
  is.
- **`*` followed by a query is 400, for `OPTIONS` as for every other
  method.** asterisk-form is exactly `*` (RFC 9112 §3.2.4). 1.2.2 refused
  `GET *` and still served `GET *?x`, and `OPTIONS *?x` reached the
  application as `*` with a query. Who: nobody known.
- **On HTTP/2, a `:path` that neither begins with `/` nor is the `*` of an
  `OPTIONS` request is a stream error** (`RST_STREAM(PROTOCOL_ERROR)`, as
  for every malformed request; the connection goes on). That is `abc`,
  `*x`, `*?q`, `*` with a method other than `OPTIONS` (which HTTP/1.1 has
  answered 400 since 1.2.2), and an absolute URI in `:path`: HTTP/2 carries
  the scheme and the authority in fields of their own, and `:path` is the
  path and query alone (RFC 9113 §8.3.1). All of these used to reach the
  application. Who: clients that build pseudo-header fields by hand.
- **On HTTP/2, a `:method` longer than 64 bytes is a stream error.**
  HTTP/1.1 has answered 501 to one since 1.2.2; a stream carried it to the
  application. The longest registered method has 17 bytes.
- **`requestTimeoutMs` and `idleTimeoutMs` act later than they did, by up to
  one sweep interval** (half the shortest timeout set, between 250 ms and
  1 s): never before the time set has passed, where they used to act up to
  one interval before it. Who: tests that expect a 408 or a close within
  the timeout itself. Do: allow two sweep intervals past it.

## What a pooled request state holds

A state holding more than 128 KiB of buffer storage is freed instead of
kept.

- When its connection comes to rest, if a request head of more than 16 KiB
  has been parsed into it since it was last measured. The state is measured
  there - every buffer it owns: the parser's, the snapshot's, the sixteen
  staging slots', the pending-input, cork and response buffers - and goes
  back to the pool only within the bound.
- At every sweep, which measures all pooled states. Heads within 16 KiB
  leave no mark, and many of them can still grow each of a state's buffers by
  some kilobytes; a state they took past the bound is freed within a sweep
  interval (at most a second) of coming to rest.
- When the sweep brings the pool back from the 256 states a burst may leave
  to 32, the states it keeps are the ones holding least. It used to keep the
  32 that had come back first, whatever they held.

So the pool holds at most 32 x 128 KiB after a sweep, and what it holds does
not depend on what a client that has gone sent. The request objects an
HTTP/2 server keeps for reuse (up to 1,024) hold a state each, whose path
and query storage a stream's `:path` grows up to `maxHeaderListSize`; they
are held to the same bound at the sweep. That retention is older than 1.2.3
and small at the default `maxHeadSize` (a stream's fields are bounded by
`maxHeaderListSize`, 64 KiB); with the limit raised it was not: a request
with a 400,000-byte `:path` left 400 KB in the object that carried it.

Measured on macOS arm64, the allocated heap of the process after 200
connections sent the requests described above and closed, and several sweeps
had run:

| | before the connections | after, 1.2.2 | after, 1.2.3 | after, 1.2.4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| batched dispatch | 2.2 MB | 2.7 MB | 27.4 MB | 2.7 MB |
| one-by-one dispatch | 2.2 MB | 2.7 MB | 15.0 MB | 2.7 MB |

The test a request pays for this is no instruction: the parser compares the
size of a complete head with 16 KiB where it compared it with `maxHeadSize`
(and tells the two apart only past 16 KiB), and the connection reads the
parser's note in the load that already asked whether the parser was idle.
A request with a head past 16 KiB pays the measurement when its connection
comes to rest: about 200 instructions, on a request that costs 59,000 to
parse (see "Performance").

## Replies dropped behind an engine route

Since 1.2.2 a cleartext connection to a server with `http2` on is closed
without a reply when its first line is neither the HTTP/2 preface nor an
HTTP/1 request line: there is no protocol to answer in. "First" was decided
by whether the connection had been given a request id or had started a
response, and a request answered by a static or parameter route takes no
request id and leaves its response in the cork buffer until the read has
been gone through. So `GET /static HTTP/1.1` followed by `GARBAGE` in the
same write was taken for a connection that had said nothing: it was closed,
and the 200 already built for the first request was dropped with it. 1.2.1
sent the 200 and a 400.

Whether a line is the first of its connection is now something the parser
knows: it is told so when the connection's first bytes turn out not to be
the HTTP/2 preface, and it forgets it with the first complete request,
whoever answers that. A connection that has had a request parsed has spoken
HTTP/1, and a line that is not HTTP/1 is answered 400 behind the responses
it is owed, as 1.2.2's note said. A first line with no HTTP-version in it
is still closed without a reply. Servers without `http2`, and TLS
listeners, were not affected.

## The end of a TLS connection

After the last response of a connection the engine half-closes and lingers:
its FIN goes out, and the socket stays open, its input discarded, until the
peer's FIN or two seconds (RFC 9112 §9.6). A server that closes outright
has the bytes the peer is still sending answered with a RST by the kernel,
and a RST can take the response with it. TLS connections were left out of
that: `close_notify`, then `close()`. So over TLS a client that was still
sending when the connection ended - the rest of an upload answered 413,
requests pipelined behind an HTTP/1.0 response that only the end of the
connection delimits, WebSocket frames behind a Close the server started,
frames behind the `GOAWAY` of an HTTP/2 connection error - got a reset, and
with Node's client lost whatever it had not read yet.

A TLS connection now ends as a plaintext one does, with the `close_notify`
ahead of the FIN.

- The bound is the plaintext one: two seconds, enforced by the same sweep;
  the connection counts against `maxConnections` meanwhile.
- Nothing the peer sends in that time is decrypted or answered: it is
  dropped before the TLS layer sees it, as plaintext input is dropped before
  the parser.
- The server does not wait for the peer's `close_notify`. The peer's FIN
  ends the wait, or the deadline.
- When the socket does not take the `close_notify` whole (its buffer is
  full), the rest is queued, input is closed at once, and the FIN follows
  when it has gone, under `responseTimeoutMs`.

With a client that keeps writing for 300 ms before it reads, on 1.2.3 the
body of the HTTP/1.0 response, the 413, the WebSocket Close and the `GOAWAY`
were each lost every time; all four arrive now (`tls-conformance`, `http2`).
A randomised stress of pipelined clients over TLS, run under ASan, reported
truncated responses in 12 of 24 runs on 1.2.3 on Linux and in 6 of 24 on
macOS. On this release it reports none in 72 runs on each of epoll,
io_uring and libuv on Linux and 72 on macOS; the plaintext runs beside them
report none either, as before.

## Receive timeouts

The sweep that enforces `idleTimeoutMs` and `requestTimeoutMs` counts its
own passes, and it counted the first pass after a connection's last byte, or
after a request's first, as a whole interval, though it may come a
millisecond later. A timeout therefore acted between one interval early and
on time. The interval is half the shortest timeout set (at most one second,
at least 250 ms), so `requestTimeoutMs: 2000` answered 408 between 1.0 and
2.0 seconds after a request's first byte, and with 1000 a request that took
800 ms to arrive was refused when its first byte fell late in an interval.
With the default of 300 seconds the error was at most one second.

Both now act on the pass after the one that completes the count: never
before the time set, and less than two intervals after it (one, when the
timeout is a whole number of intervals). `responseTimeoutMs` already started
its count from a pass of its own and is unchanged.

## The buffer budget

`maxBodySize`, `maxPendingBytes`, `wsMaxMessageSize`, the write high-water
mark and an HTTP/2 session's own bound each limit what one connection may
make the server hold. A server accepts as many connections as it has
descriptors for, and the sum of those limits over them is not a bound on
anything: 32,768 peers that each send 889 KB of a form, or a 4 MB multipart
body, or a megabyte of compressed data, or a WebSocket message in fragments
without an end, or each ask over HTTP/2 for sixty-four responses with a
window of zero, are all within every limit, and together they ask for tens
of gigabytes.

So the engine counts what its connections hold in buffers for their peers -
requests still arriving, responses not yet taken - per thread, against the
thread's share of a budget for the process (`memoryBudget`; unset, an eighth
of the memory the process may use, divided among the threads that have a
server listening and never below the largest single message a thread's
servers accept). Over its share, a connection whose read has just ended
with more than 16 KiB of requests still arriving is closed, with the
protocol's own word for it (503, 1013, `GOAWAY`). The 16 KiB are what the
peer has sent, not the storage it is in, and a connection's own unread
response is not held against its next request, so small requests are served
while the budget is full. Response bytes are counted and close nothing
where they are queued: a response to a peer that reads it is delivered
whatever its size against the share. At the sweep, while less than an
eighth of a share is free, connections whose requests or unread responses
have not moved for a whole interval are closed, unless a handler holds
their request: a peer that fills the budget and then stops would otherwise
keep it full for the five minutes of the default timeouts. A queue that
shrinks, a completed write, a read or an opened HTTP/2 window is movement,
whatever `responseTimeoutMs` is set to, and so is the peer acknowledging
bytes the kernel holds for it (asked of the kernel, on Linux and macOS, only
for a connection about to be closed by this rule).
`docs/API.md` ("Memory") has the rules in full, and `docs/DESIGN.md` why
connections are closed rather than made to wait.

On Windows the response side of this counts less than elsewhere, for a
reason that is not new: Winsock takes the first two sends outstanding on a
socket whole, whatever their size, and tells the engine they are complete.
A response the system took that way is in no queue of the engine's, so it is
not counted and no peer is closed for it by the budget (`idleTimeoutMs`
closes a peer that never reads); from the third send outstanding on a
connection, and for requests still arriving, the budget is the same there.

A request that is answered within the read that brought it - every request
of a handler that answers before it returns - pays nothing for this: its
connection is at rest when the read ends and has nothing counted. A
connection a read leaves awake is counted there when it holds bytes of its
peer's or for it (on HTTP/2, when the session says its figures have moved),
and at the sweep, which counts everything, otherwise.

## A loop that falls behind

(Linux, the epoll transport.) A loop turn served every socket that had
something to read. What a service costs is what the socket's bytes ask for,
and with thousands of sockets ready - each with a read buffer of pipelined
requests, or a body whose handler takes milliseconds - one turn took
seconds: no timer ran in it, no connection was accepted, and a client that
had just connected was not read from. Nothing was exhausted; the server was
simply not there.

A turn now has a slice of 50 ms. A turn that fits is served as before. One
that does not ends there, and until the loop has caught up the sockets that
are ready wait on two queues that every turn serves: the ones that were not
served in the turn before (a new connection, a quiet one) first, within half
the slice and from both ends of their queue, the oldest before the newest;
the ones that were, in turn, for the rest. The oldest socket of each queue
is served in every turn whatever it costs, so no connection waits for ever
behind later ones, and none is timed out for the time its socket waited: a
sweep counts nothing against a socket that has not had a turn since the
sweep before, and pushes the response queued on it, so that
`responseTimeoutMs` closes the peer that is not reading and no other.
While sockets wait, a read takes less than the whole buffer -
64 KiB shrinking to 1 KiB as the number waiting passes 256 - and the
listener takes up to 1,024 connections a turn instead of 64, so that a
round over everything that waits stays short however much there is. A
waiting socket is not polled until its turn (with 16,000 waiting, being
told about them again in every poll took as long as serving), and a
connection nothing is read from any more - lingering, or closing behind an
error response - has its input discarded in the kernel instead of copied
out.

`probe().turns` is `{ cut, waiting }`; both are zero on a loop that keeps
up. Its turns of fewer than 32 sockets are served by the loop that served
them on 1.2.3, and its larger ones read the clock once per 32 sockets
served.

## Measured against floods

HttpArena's resilience suite runs a server in a container of 2 CPUs and
4 GiB and attacks it with 32,768 connections for up to 45 seconds, 41
attacks in turn, a fresh container for each. A `GET` on a new connection
once a second, with two seconds to answer, says whether the server is still
there; the container's memory high-water mark and whether the kernel killed
it say the rest. The server is MoroJS on this engine with default limits:
1.9.2 on 1.2.3, and the MoroJS release that follows on 1.2.4 (which also
refuses a urlencoded form of more than 1,000 fields, the `hashdos` row's
cost per request). Run on Linux arm64 under Docker, the attack client on ten
other CPUs; these are the twelve attacks that hit 1.2.3 hardest:

| Attack | 1.2.3 | 1.2.4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `tiny-chunk-flood` | survived, 10% of probes failed, 2,557 MiB | survived, no probe failed, 2,025 MiB |
| `pipelining-flood` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 2,410 MiB |
| `decompression-bomb` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 1,976 MiB |
| `decomp-codec-bypass` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 1,958 MiB |
| `hashdos` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 1,810 MiB |
| `json-nesting-bomb` | 94% of probes failed, 875 MiB | survived, no probe failed, 2,491 MiB |
| `multipart-flood` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 2,124 MiB |
| `percent-decode-quadratic` | 74% of probes failed, 493 MiB | survived, no probe failed, 846 MiB |
| `json-bignum` | 50% of probes failed, 4,096 MiB | survived, no probe failed, 2,446 MiB |
| `window-stall` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 1,161 MiB |
| `ws-fragment-bomb` | OOM-killed | survived, no probe failed, 2,287 MiB |
| `ws-pong-flood` | survived, no probe failed, 3,983 MiB | survived, no probe failed, 2,312 MiB |

On 1.2.4 all 41 attacks end with the server running and no probe failed in
any of them; the highest memory mark is 2,491 MiB (`json-nesting-bomb`).
Of that memory, the engine's own buffers are held to the budget, 512 MiB
here (plus the 16 KiB a connection may hold without being refused); the
process as a whole reached 1.3 GiB, and most of the rest is what the kernel
holds for 32,768 sockets - 1.8 GiB at the most, by the container's memory
statistics - which the engine does not bound; `maxConnections` does.

Timed more finely, a `GET` on a new connection every 250 ms during
`pipelining-flood` (each connection sending 10,000 requests at a time and
reading nothing): 5 to 7 ms before the flood, 716 ms at the worst while all
32,768 connections were arriving at once, 65 to 130 ms once they had. On
1.2.3 four probes in five went unanswered until the container was killed.

Throughput without a flood is what it was (the same container, six CPUs for
the server and six for the load generator, 256 connections, five alternating
runs of ten seconds each): 748,000 requests a second against 761,000 on
1.2.3 for a plain `GET` through MoroJS, 213,000 against 206,000 over
cleartext HTTP/2, where single runs of either differ by 3%.

## Added

- `memoryBudget` (serve option, `capabilities.bufferBudget`): bytes the
  process may hold in buffers for its peers, all connections together.
  Unset: an eighth of the memory the process may use. `Infinity`: no budget.
- `probe().bufferBudget`: `{ held, limit, shed, evicted }` for the calling
  thread - the bytes counted, the thread's share, and the connections
  closed for it where a read ended (their requests did not fit) and at the
  sweep (nothing of theirs had moved).
- `probe().turns`: `{ cut, waiting }` for the calling thread's loop - turns
  that ended on their time slice, and sockets waiting for a later turn.
- `MORO_ENGINE_SERVE_DEBUG=1` also logs, every quarter of a second while
  sockets wait, the longest turn and the number waiting.
- `probe().requestStates`: `{ pooled, bytes }`, the request states the
  servers of the calling thread keep for the requests to come, and the heap
  storage their buffers hold. Absent on engines before 1.2.4.

On epoll the rows of one request per loop turn are 41 to 43 instructions
shorter than on 1.2.3, 3.5% of a static route: a turn of one socket no
longer goes through the sort that puts a turn's sockets in order, and what
the time slice costs a turn that fits it is one compare (a turn of fewer
than 32 sockets is served by the loop that served it on 1.2.3). On libuv
every row but four is a few instructions shorter than on 1.2.3 as well.

The buffer budget costs a request that is answered within its read nothing:
its connection is at rest when the read ends, and whether anything is
counted for it is asked with a load the read already made. A request that
leaves its connection awake pays for a look at whether there is anything to
count: an HTTP/2 request 6 instructions on libuv (0.16%), a connection
opened for a single request 11 (0.28%); on epoll both rows are below 1.2.3
by what the turn saves. The request with a 21 KiB head pays for the
